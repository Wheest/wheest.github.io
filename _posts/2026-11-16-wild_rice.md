In this post, I chat with Margaret Gibson (aka, my maw), who's had a long career
across a range of sectors. Today we're going back to the start of it: setting up
Wild Rice, a vegetarian ready-meal business, in Glasgow in 1986, when she was 23
and a vegetarian section on a menu didn't exist.

<!--more-->

---

**Today I want to ask about your time running Wild Rice, which might set the
scene for some stuff we talk about in future. But first, talk us a wee bit about
yourself.**

_My name is Margaret Gibson, and I've had a long and very varied career in lots
of different sectors._

_I should probably start from where the inspiration came from. In 1984 or 85, I
decided to take on a lot of jobs, earn as much money as possible, save up, and
go away travelling. Nowadays that's called a gap year, but I was a council house
kid from an estate. I just really liked the idea of travelling a little bit,
learning a bit of a language, and having a different experience. So I had about
four jobs on the go._

**What kind of things were you doing?**

_I worked in an art centre, the
[Third Eye Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_for_Contemporary_Arts).
I worked in a bar. I did some cleaning at one point, babysitting, just trying to
save as much as I could. I also signed up for night classes in Italian, and
ended up studying for what was then an O grade. So I got the basics of the
language and went off to Italy._

_While I was away, I started to eat less and less meat. I was really impressed
by just how delicious simple ingredients were without meat added to them: lovely
roasted vegetables, or courgette flowers stuffed with ricotta. Things I'd never
even imagined existed. I was also training for a full marathon that summer, so I
was getting up at 6am to run before the heat of the day, and then I came back to
run the Glasgow Marathon at the end of September._

_When I came back, a girl I'd been at school with approached me and said she was
thinking about setting up a small business. She'd become really interested in
vegetarianism, and I said, well, that's quite strange, I have as well. So at
that point I decided to cut meat out of my diet completely and became a
lacto-ovo vegetarian._

**What was that like in Glasgow in the 80s? Were people like, "wit ye daein'"?**

_It was a lot more difficult, let's put it that way. If you ever had enough
money to go out for dinner, it would have been some sort of lasagne or a cheese
salad. Ethnic food was a bit easier, you could always get a vegetable curry, but
there was never a vegetarian section in a menu. It just didn't exist._

_So we spent about three months, from the end of September, doing loads of
research, writing business plans, speaking to banks, finding any grants we could
apply for, and finding somewhere to set the business up. When I look back now,
we did it with so little experience, and with the innocence of youth, perhaps
not quite realising the challenges ahead. We signed a lease on a workshop and
kitted it all out. We'd got a couple of grants because we were under 25, and my
mum and Linda's mum, Linda being the girl I set the business up with, each gave
us a thousand pounds as a loan._

**A thousand pounds then, that's not nothing, even nowadays.**

_The reason my mum, a factory worker, had a thousand pounds was that she'd been
made redundant from her factory job._

**Would this have been White Horse, aye?**

_White Horse whisky, yeah. So it wasn't like my mum had thousands sitting in the
bank. It was a really big thing for them to do, and a really big amount of faith
in us when you consider we were 23._

_We bought a lot of second-hand equipment, including stainless steel work tables
and benches from a bakery that was closing down. We rented a power washer, and
when we turned them upside down they were covered in maggots. It just hadn't
been a very clean bakery, which maybe explains why it closed down._

**I suppose their failure was your success.**

_Yes, because we got them for next to nothing. We power washed them all, and
then we had really good quality materials to work with. We perfected recipes and
did a lot of market research. We asked local health food shops if we could do
tastings, and that gave us a lot of really good feedback._

_What we found was that people did want healthy vegetarian food, but just like
everybody else at that time, they wanted to come home and stick something in the
oven. It was the early days of ready meals. If you'd forgotten to soak your
beans, it's quite a lot of work. Whereas we were saying, here is a really
healthy ready meal with no sugar, no preservatives, no additives at all. It's
basically homemade._

_We got the business up and running on the 6th of January 1986, and quite
quickly it did really well. But we'd take orders and hand deliver them, so two
or three days a week you'd be taking a dozen lasagnes here, twelve chillies
there, and eventually that wasn't very cost effective. We needed to spend the
time making the food, not delivering it._

**So what changed?**

_The challenge was we didn't have much of a safety net. When we took a dozen
lasagnes to somebody, we got paid for them there and then, so we were getting
money in every day. We couldn't afford to put a freezer van on the road, but
could we deal with a distributor that had freezer vans and could sell our
products? Again, with that innocence of youth, we didn't think anything of
picking up the phone and asking if we could come and speak to people, and we got
taken on by quite a well-established frozen food distributor._

_They were quite demanding about the quality of the packaging and labelling, so
it cost us quite a lot of money to start working with them. And their terms of
trade were 30 days, which meant we had to wait 30 days to be paid._

**So you'd be 30 days in the hole before the money came in?**

_And we didn't really have much of a cushion to tide us over, so we started on
the back foot. When I look back now, it was too soon. What we should have done
was build up a nest egg of capital to take us to that next stage of growth. But
we were very naive in many ways._

_It would have been okay if that company were good at paying at 30 days, but
they weren't. Bigger companies so often put pressures on small businesses that
they don't realise. You'd spend two weeks after the 30 days phoning, trying to
get somebody from accounts to sort out the invoice, and suddenly it would go to
60 days, sometimes 90. And if you started sending lawyers' letters, that's
eating into your profits._

_So it became quite a stressful time in my life. But we persevered and built up
a fairly substantial business, and at one point we had four employees. Which is
a different kind of stress as well._

**Right, because folk are paying the rent and filling their bellies off the
thing you're running. If you mess up, it impacts other families.**

_Yes, definitely. And bear in mind we were growing as people too, and I think we
both realised we were quite different. My business partner was in a very
long-term relationship that wasn't going well, and her heart was very much on to
a new relationship and wanting to do something different. So after a couple of
years, she left the business and went off travelling herself._

_However, again, that naivety. I let her go, but we were in a lot of debt. We
had borrowed a lot of money to survive and to buy new equipment as the business
grew. She would probably have said, oh yeah, but I handed you the business. But
the business at that time..._

**...wasn't an asset.**

_No, it wasn't an asset. I took that on, and that caused a lot of stress for me
over the following few years._

_However, it taught me an awful lot about business. I started to realise I was
pretty good at looking at trends, at the whole marketing of things, and I was
good with people. I started getting asked by a lot of schools, colleges, and
universities to come in and speak to students about what it's like to set up in
business as a young entrepreneur, the pitfalls and the advantages. And the more
I did that, the more I realised I had quite an unusual skill set._

**What do you mean?**

_When you're younger, you tend to have fewer responsibilities. The older you
get, you might have a family, a mortgage, car payments. My life was quite small
in terms of commitments, so I had an opportunity a lot of people 20 years older
than me didn't have. And I learned an awful lot about people, how to manage
people, and the opportunities and challenges of establishing a small business.
You could probably read about them in a university programme, but actually
living them gives you a daily experience of the world of work._

**You can read about it, but what you actually learn is how it relates to who
you are as a person.**

_Yeah, definitely. And from the business point of view, we did win quite a lot
of prestigious awards. We won the Scottish
[Shell LiveWIRE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_LiveWIRE) competition,
which at the time was a really big thing. You went down to London with all your
expenses covered, put up in a lovely hotel, with trips to the theatre and
beautiful meals. It felt very glamorous. It wasn't the first time I'd been to
London, but it was certainly the first time I'd been able to have a nice
experience like that._

_There were something like 20 or 25 finalists from all over the UK, and we came
runners up. The prize was presented by the Lord Mayor of London, and you got a
nice photograph and stuff like that._

_I also started getting asked to judge business competitions, which is something
I've taken right through. I'm the longest-serving assessor for the
[Scottish EDGE](https://scottishedge.com/) fund, and I've judged every round
apart from the pilot. Twice a year I read a dozen or so business applications
and then interview the founders. So that grounding I had myself 40 years ago,
and the experience I've built on since, I'm still using. It was a very
challenging time in my life, but I certainly don't regret it._

<aside style="border-left: 3px solid #bbb; margin: 1.5em 0; padding: 0.4em 1em; font-size: 0.92em; color: #444;">
Scottish EDGE has run since 2012, backed by the Hunter Foundation, the Royal Bank
of Scotland, the Scottish Government and Scottish Enterprise. It bills itself as
Scotland's biggest business funding competition, and has
<a href="https://scottishedge.com/">awarded £32.7m to 788 businesses</a>.
</aside>

**Vegetarianism was pretty fresh in the Scottish zeitgeist, it was a woman-run
business, and maybe there was a class component too. Were there any challenges
along those dimensions that stuck out to you? It's hard for me to imagine what
the 80s were like.**

_Bear in mind it was just after the punk era. I knew a lot of people, myself
included, who used punk to express ourselves, whether that was singing in a
band, which I did, or playing instruments, setting up fanzines, setting up music
labels. There was a sense of young people coming together to change things. It
felt quite a dynamic time to be young, actually._

_I didn't particularly feel there was a lot of sexism. There was a bit of being
patronised, but you could probably ask 22 or 23 year olds now if that still
happens, and of course it does. People still patronise young people. They
perhaps don't realise they do it. The social class thing, I probably wasn't
aware of, but then I wasn't particularly mixing with people that were very
wealthy._

**That's interesting. When I [interviewed Glenn
Gibson]({% post_url 2026-02-03-post_punk_press_glenn_gibson %}), he talked about
that "you can do it too" spirit too. It seemed to really energise that
generation.**

_Yeah. And sometimes, sitting here now, it's really easy to think the world's in
a bad place. But actually, the majority of people I meet are good and decent
people. There's a small percentage that are not, and unfortunately they're
probably quite vocal. But most people just want to live a good and decent life
with..._

**...some good scran.**

_Good scran, yeah._

**Looking back on Wild Rice, what was your best day, and your worst?**

_The best day was probably winning the Scottish Shell LiveWIRE competition. It
was in the [Mitchell Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell_Library),
at the library in Glasgow, with a really big audience, and it just felt really
special. A real high moment in my life. I've still got the physical award, and
we got a lot of press coverage from it. It was nice for my parents to see their
girl in the Daily Record or the Herald, the newspapers they read._

**Aye, makes it real.**

_And probably my mum felt a bit more secure about her thousand pounds, which I
have to say was paid back in full. My mum used to come and visit the
manufacturing unit and help out, washing or chopping vegetables. When my nephew
Graeme was born, she looked after him while his mum was at work, so he'd quite
often come in in his pram. He loved coming to the unit. We had a potato peeler
machine, quite noisy, where you poured the potatoes in and the water ran through
and they all came out peeled. He loved opening the door when all the potatoes
fell out into the big bucket. That was his job, until he was about four or five
and started school._

**And the worst day?**

_Probably when I got a red bill for the electricity and I was thinking, I don't
have any money, I've got food in the freezer, and if they cut the electricity
off, I don't know what I'm going to do. I obviously found the money, because I
paid the bill, but I was so upset by it that I've blocked out where I got the
money from. Possibly my mum, or maybe I sold something._

**Did you ever have to shake someone down?**

_No. I have been quite angry with people for not paying invoices, right enough.
It's the injustice. You've put a lot of love and care and attention into the
food, put it into really nice packaging which you've spent a lot of money on,
delivered it, they've sold it, and then they don't pay you for it._

**Anyone that still owes you money? We could go out after we've had our chat.**

_No, no. When I eventually decided it was time to stop the business, I needed a
bit more stability in my life. I'd like to do something different. I was doing a
lot of guest speaking, and one of the colleges I'd studied a communications
qualification at had set up an enterprise unit. The business advisor I'd had a
lot of advice from said they'd really like me to join their team, because a lot
of their students would relate to me as a young person. He knew I was in a
difficult position financially, and a wee bit at a crossroads, so he offered me
a job._

_So I wound the company up. I sold as much as I could, and whenever I got money
in, I paid any outstanding invoices. The only one left to pay back was the bank,
and I agreed a repayment plan. I knew it would take about three years to repay
the loan and the overdraft. But it felt like, okay, it's been an expensive
apprenticeship, but I wouldn't have got the job I went on to do if I hadn't done
what I'd done._

_In retrospect, it was a great launch pad for the next 20 years of my career. I
worked in the enterprise unit at the college, as it was then, for two years, and
then took a job that lasted 20 years. So it was a special time._

**There's a lot more to discuss, and other directions to go in, but we've had
quite a big chat there. To finish: what's your favourite food?**

_I actually really love chickpeas. They've just got such a delicious texture and
taste, and they're so versatile: through a stew, mashed up for hummus, made into
flour for flatbreads. I really like chickpeas through a curry. Low fat, high
protein, just really nutritious._

**What's your thought on calling them garbanzo beans?**

_Well, if that's what they're called in your country or your culture, you stick
with it. But when I was introduced to them, they were chickpeas, so they'll
always be chickpeas in my mind. But if that's not your name for them, I'm not
going to fight with you._

**Well, thanks very much for your time, Margaret.**
